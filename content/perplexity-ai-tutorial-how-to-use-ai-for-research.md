> **TL;DR:** Perplexity AI combines an AI chatbot with web search, so instead of ten blue links I get a ready-made answer built from several cited sources. I compare it with Google and with ChatGPT on a real question about the UFC 300 fight, where ChatGPT hallucinated and Perplexity was accurate. Then I show Focus mode, PDF attachments and Collections, which let me save a prompt and get research back in a set format.

I'm Alfie Marsh, and this is a full tutorial of Perplexity AI.

## What is Perplexity AI?

*[Watch from 0:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N3lNv9CT9RE&t=0s)*

Perplexity is an AI-powered search engine and research platform that gives you accurate answers to comprehensive and pretty complex questions. It was founded in August 2022 by its CEO, who was previously at OpenAI and DeepMind.

It has quickly gained traction. They raised $100 million last year in a Series B, and I think it was a $520 million valuation. They have notable investors including Jeff Bezos, Nvidia and Databricks. As of January 2024 they had 10 million monthly active users, and if you fast forward to today, which is April, they have 60 million monthly visitors on their website, I believe, if you check using Similarweb. It's crazy traction within a very short period of time.

## An answer engine, not ten blue links

*[Watch from 1:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N3lNv9CT9RE&t=62s)*

In a nutshell, Perplexity combines the capabilities of an AI chatbot with web search, so that you get ready-made answers. A lot of people talk about it as an AI-powered search engine, but its CEO often refers to it as an answer engine. It works pretty differently.

Take the question "how many jobs will be displaced by AI?" If I ask Google, we get some links. It does a pretty decent job of summarising some information within a particular post, and it gives you the questions people asked, but basically it's ten blue links.

In Perplexity the answer is different. It first asked me which industries I'm interested in; I skipped that. It then tries to understand the question and looks for sources. If I open up its three steps, I can see it searched the web with different keywords, such as jobs displaced by AI, AI impact on job loss and AI job displacement by industry. It's optimising the search itself to answer my question. It found 20 sources, and some are cited right at the top so I can click in directly.

What's more interesting is the answer itself. It takes those sources and aims to answer my question using the content within them. Google's summary is just giving me the exact answer to my question, which is hidden within one particular article. Now imagine your question is complex and no single article mentions the answer, because it's discovered across multiple sources. Google isn't going to be able to do that, but Perplexity takes the aggregate information and provides a well-rounded answer. Here it pulled in statistics, such as Goldman Sachs saying 300 million people or full-time jobs worldwide could be impacted, and McKinsey reports. They're all cited, and I can access the original source directly. That makes it really powerful for answering questions, not just searching the internet.

## How it differs from ChatGPT

*[Watch from 4:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N3lNv9CT9RE&t=268s)*

On ChatGPT 3.5, which you have access to for free, I asked who won the Max Holloway versus Justin Gaethje fight at UFC 300 last weekend. Spoiler alert: Max Holloway won. ChatGPT said that as of its last update in January 2022 the two hadn't fought.

The problem with language models like ChatGPT is that they are trained on a static data set. Once trained, the model predicts the answer to your question based on what it has already learned. If you ask a factual question it doesn't have the answer to, it can't answer, or it might just hallucinate and make something up. Its training was cut off in January 2022, it is now April 2024, and that fight isn't in the training data.

Google will answer, because I can see the fight result, but it just gives me the links that have the answer. It's a search engine helping me find websites, not something that directly answers my question.

In Perplexity, I asked again. It pulled the sources, including YouTube and Reddit, with images on the right, and said that based on the search results Max Holloway defeated Justin Gaethje at UFC 300 to win the BMF title, which is true. It gives me the actual sources, so it behaves very differently.

## Testing ChatGPT 4 on the same question

*[Watch from 6:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N3lNv9CT9RE&t=412s)*

On ChatGPT 4, the Pro version, previously it wouldn't have been able to answer this question and would have had to search the internet. They are starting to provide these answers, and it did go and search the internet. It told me Max Holloway won with a knockout victory, but when I asked which round, I wanted to see if it was hallucinating, and it was: it said the first round, and that the judges didn't score the fight. That's factually incorrect. He won in the fifth round, knocking him out in the last two seconds of the round.

I also don't think the latest model was trained up until that weekend, so it must have been searching the internet. When I asked a follow-up in Perplexity, it said that with just one second remaining in the fifth and final round, which is accurate.

So it's much better at pulling real-time information from the internet, and it helps me do research without believing in hallucinations. ChatGPT and models like Claude and Gemini have a trained model that gives you an answer that may not be factually correct, because it's just predicting what it should say. They are detached from the real world.

Perplexity is actually powered by those very same language models. I can change the model that's used, from GPT-4 to Claude and so on. The difference is one step in between: it searches for the articles, reads and understands them, and then aggregates them into an answer. ChatGPT is starting to do this with Bing, through its partnership with Microsoft, but it's pretty basic and doesn't give you this kind of aggregated answer.

## Focus mode and attaching PDFs

*[Watch from 10:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N3lNv9CT9RE&t=648s)*

Focus mode lets me specify which types of sources I want to use. For example, I could focus on Reddit and ask for the top AI tools for marketers in 2024. It understands the question, searches the web and pulls results. Here it did one search and found eight sources, all Reddit threads, then summarised them. It gave me types of tools and some actual ones, which is a good overview, and I can continue asking follow-up questions. If you want to narrow down to particular sources, that's a great way to do it.

I can also attach a PDF, very similar to ChatGPT and Claude, and then ask it questions. That's great for research papers if you want to summarise specific information. I think this feature is okay. It's become table stakes, since the other chats built on top of base models like ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini can do it, but it is good for pulling in your own information as opposed to just the internet.

## Collections

*[Watch from 12:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N3lNv9CT9RE&t=769s)*

Collections live in the Library. I'll look at one I've already made, called "competitive blog research". It has the same search box, but now it has a title and a description, because this search has been edited to perform a very specific type of task.

The point of this tool is to help me take a blog post and analyse it. If I want to rank for a particular keyword, I want to see what other people are ranking for, and I want the research to come back in a very specific format. When you edit a collection, you give it a name, a description and a prompt. Instead of just answering the question you type into the search bar, it answers it within the context of that prompt.

My prompt says: you are an SEO research assistant who reviews articles that compete for your targeted keyword. Analyse the link provided, output the keywords the post is ranking for, outline the structure and headings of the post, provide a list of all the key research links the post references, then provide information gaps the post could be improved on, and provide research links to reference other sources we could now reference.

What I'm trying to do is find a keyword I want to rank for, see who else is ranking for it, analyse what they're doing well and make sure I include that in my post, then look for information gaps where I could improve on it. I've been very specific about the way it outputs the results.

I tried it on a post from Spendesk, the company I used to work for. It started to produce the keywords and a post outline, which is great. It said the post doesn't reference links to any other website or sources, which I think is incorrect; when I opened the post in another tab, it definitely references plenty. The information gaps section is good, since it tells me what's missing and how else I could level up the post. It also gave research links for new topics, but it hasn't been smart enough to pull out the links in the page itself.

I want those because if the piece said, for example, that 73% of X did Y, I'd like to know where they got that research so I could reference it in my own post. It might also help with internal link building, because I could go to that person and say we're now ranking for this and we're more comprehensive than the last piece you were featured in, would you like to link back to us?

That's what Collections is: it lets me do a particular type of search and get research back in a particular format without repeating that again and again.

## Where Perplexity stands

*[Watch from 17:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N3lNv9CT9RE&t=1040s)*

That's pretty much it. It's quite a simple platform, and very powerful. The biggest question I have is what Google is going to do to combat this, and what ChatGPT is going to do. They both have the power to index the web and provide these sorts of results, so I wonder where the competitive edge between Perplexity, Google and OpenAI's ChatGPT is going to be.

For the time being, Perplexity AI is a very powerful research tool for getting past hallucinations and providing real-world citations with real information that you can answer from quickly.
