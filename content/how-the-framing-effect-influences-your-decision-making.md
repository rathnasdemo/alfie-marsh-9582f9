> **TL;DR:** The framing effect is about how information is presented, not what is presented, and it changes the emotional response and the decision. I cover association, comparative frames, frame inversion and changing the reference point, with Starbucks and Red Bull as examples. Then I use Daniel Kahneman's loss aversion and the vaccination example to show that framing an option as a loss steers people away from it, and that a different question can change the answer.

Did you know that our decisions and emotions can be influenced simply by the way information is presented to us? This is known as the framing effect, and here is how it works.

## What the framing effect is

*[Watch from 0:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x1uCimKtPyY&t=0s)*

The framing effect explains how we can present the same information in different ways and evoke a different emotional response. That can drastically impact our decision-making ability, and sometimes the framing is done consciously and sometimes it isn't. If you want to be an independent thinker, have more control over your decisions and be able to influence other people, the framing effect is a critical mental model to learn.

Just like a frame around a painting, a frame can draw attention to something. In the frame I'm in right now, I'm being framed by two lights, the background is slightly blurrier and there's light on my face, which draws attention to me. Framing helps you draw attention to something you want your audience to see.

Framing is not about what you present, it's about how you present it. If I put on a green light, maybe that would accentuate the colour in my eyes; if I turn the light to red, it may bring out the red patches in my skin. How you frame an image or a piece of information really controls how it's perceived.

## Association and comparative frames

*[Watch from 1:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x1uCimKtPyY&t=76s)*

One way to use framing is through association. Take the text "I'll be waiting for you". It can have many different meanings depending on the situation. One font gives the impression of a murderer waiting to kill you, while the other looks like something you'd write in a card: "I can't wait to have you home, I'm going to be waiting for you." It's exactly the same piece of information, but the framing is different. Through association, you link one type of font with something negative and the other with something positive.

Comparative frames are also powerful. Take two circles. Depending on what information you surround a circle with, it can seem larger or smaller. Both circles are exactly the same size, but the information around them determines the interpretation. The lessons from visual framing can be a really powerful base for interpreting information, but there are many other techniques for framing information that can affect someone's decisions.

## Frame inversion

*[Watch from 2:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x1uCimKtPyY&t=138s)*

Frame inversion shifts the perspective on a piece of information by flipping it on its head. A yogurt could be described as 20% fat or 80% fat free. It's exactly the same information, since 20% fat means 80% fat free and vice versa. Most of the time yogurt brands focus on 80% fat free, because they want our attention drawn to the absence of fat rather than the presence of it.

## Reframing the reference point

*[Watch from 2:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x1uCimKtPyY&t=171s)*

Another powerful technique is to reframe the reference point. Say a Starbucks coffee costs five bucks. Compared with 10 cents for making coffee at home, that seems ridiculously expensive. But change the reference point: compare the five dollar Starbucks with renting an office space in a WeWork that includes a free coffee for a hundred bucks a day. Suddenly the five bucks becomes ridiculously cheap. Simply switching the reference point can change the perception of something.

Another famous example is Coca-Cola and Red Bull. Coca-Cola is one of the biggest brands in the world and one of the most successful soft drinks. Red Bull has also seen a ton of success, but when it first started out and was being imported into the West from Thailand, they hired a research agency to see how good the drink was. The agency reported that, from all their studies, it was the worst performing drink they had ever researched. The taste was disgusting and it had a bad side effect: it made people feel jittery. Compared with a soft drink, why would anyone want to drink something that tastes so disgusting?

Now use the reframing effect and move Red Bull from a soft drink to a functional drink. It becomes a caffeinated drink that gives you wings, associated with extreme sports and enhanced performance. The smaller can becomes a sign of potency, and the odd taste is a bit like funky medicine that's going to have some sort of side effect. Switching that frame from a soft drink to a functional drink is a really powerful way to influence perception.

## Loss versus gain

*[Watch from 4:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x1uCimKtPyY&t=278s)*

Once you know the techniques for reframing information, the next step is what to focus on. One key idea is discussed in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* by Daniel Kahneman, a psychologist who earned a Nobel Prize in economics: loss versus gain. Human beings are really focused on loss aversion. We don't like losing, so we try to maximise gains and run away from losses as much as possible.

Two of Kahneman's findings matter here:

- **A loss feels bigger than the equal gain.** If you lose 50 bucks, it's seen as much more negative than gaining 50 bucks.
- **Sure things beat probabilities.** A surefire gain is much preferred to a probable one, and a probable loss is much preferred to a surefire loss.

The key takeaway is that if there's an option you want someone not to choose, it's much more powerful to reframe it as a guaranteed loss.

## The vaccination example

*[Watch from 5:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x1uCimKtPyY&t=340s)*

Kahneman gives an example in his book. Imagine 600 people in the United States affected by an outbreak of a virus, with two options. In the first scenario we focus on the gain. Option A is a surefire win: 200 people's lives will be saved. Option B is a one-third possibility that everybody's life will be saved. Which would be most popular? With the frame on saving lives, most people chose option A because it was a surefire gain.

Now flip it and focus on the loss. Option A guarantees that 400 people die, whereas option B has a two-thirds chance that everybody dies, which means a one-third chance that everybody could survive. Reframed as a loss, most people would choose option B, because they're trying to avoid absolute loss, and that's why they don't choose option A, where 400 people are guaranteed to die. The key learning is that if you want someone not to choose an option, you should frame it as a loss.

In a sales environment, a lot of people pitching a product or service try to focus on the guaranteed, surefire wins someone will get from using it. That doesn't work well because people don't believe you when you say they're going to get a certain result. It's not a guaranteed win, it's a probable win. A better approach is to frame the status quo as a surefire loss.

## Bonus: ask a different question

*[Watch from 7:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x1uCimKtPyY&t=434s)*

Those are the top techniques for how to frame and what to focus on, but here's a bonus: if you want a different answer, you need to ask a different question.

I recently read an article about a barista at Starbucks who completely changed her sales in the shop. Normally, when you've asked for your coffee and go to pay, they ask "is that everything you wanted today?" Your automatic answer is yes, because you think they're trying to sell you something. Let's pay and go.

One time, this barista reframed the question at the checkout and said, "What else sounds delicious to you today?" That question is completely different. Answering it isn't committing to buying something, and it isn't committing to a loss of money because you've been caught in a transaction you didn't want. You're simply saying, well, I guess the lemon cake looks really nice. The next thing you know, the lemon cake is in the basket and you're paying for something you weren't originally going to have, simply because the question was reframed.
